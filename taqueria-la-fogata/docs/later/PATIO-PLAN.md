# The Patio: 20 Seats, Built Cheap, Built Legal

The patio is the biggest check you'll write in this project and the asset that keeps paying after it's written. Two rules govern everything here:

1. **The patio must be inside your licensed premises from day one.** (See the sequencing trap in [LIQUOR-LICENSE-KENNESAW.md](./LIQUOR-LICENSE-KENNESAW.md). This is the mistake that costs the most.)
2. **Every dollar you don't spend building it is a dollar you don't have to earn back.** Your revenue forecast is a guess. Your build cost is a choice. Control the thing you control.

---

## Design targets

| | |
|---|---|
| Seats | 20 (15 if the space is tight — don't force it) |
| Footprint | ~400 sq ft (allow ~18–20 sq ft per seat including circulation) |
| Tables | 5 four-tops, or 4 four-tops + 2 two-tops for flexibility |
| Shade | **Umbrellas first.** A pergola is 3–5× the cost and may trigger a structural review. |
| Boundary | Railing or fence — this is the legal edge of your licensed premises, not decoration |
| Surface | Pavers if the grade allows (cheaper, DIY-able, repairable); concrete if it doesn't |
| Season | ~March–November in Georgia. Nine usable months. |

### Why 20 and not 40

Because a full 20-seat patio looks like a hit and an half-empty 40-seat patio looks like a failure — to customers driving past and to you. Occupancy is the signal people read. Build to the size you can reliably fill at 4pm on a Wednesday, and add a second phase later if you're turning people away. A patio you can extend is worth more than a patio you overbuilt.

## Budget — and where to cut

| Item | Cheap | Mid | Nice | Can you cut it? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Survey, site plan, engineering | $1,500 | $3,000 | $5,000 | **No.** The city requires it. |
| Surface (400 sq ft) | $3,200 pavers | $4,000 | $4,800 concrete | Partially — pavers over concrete |
| Railing / fence (~90 lin ft) | $3,150 | $4,200 | $5,400 | **No.** It's your licensed boundary. |
| Shade | $1,500 umbrellas | $4,000 | $8,000 pergola | **Yes — biggest single saving** |
| Furniture, 20 seats | $2,000 used | $3,500 | $5,000 | **Yes.** Restaurant auctions, closures. |
| Lighting + electrical | $1,500 | $2,500 | $3,500 | Partially — string lights on one circuit |
| Permits + inspections | $500 | $1,200 | $2,000 | **No.** |
| Drainage / ADA path / grading | $500 | $1,500 | $3,000 | **No.** ADA is federal. |
| **Total** | **$13,850** | **$23,900** | **$36,700** | |

**Build the "cheap" column, and put the difference in the bank as working capital.** Umbrellas and used furniture on a well-built pad with proper railing is a patio people love. A pergola doesn't sell one extra margarita.

The three places people waste money on restaurant patios, in order: a pergola or permanent roof nobody asked for, new furniture when restaurant auctions exist, and outdoor heaters that get used nine days a year. Skip all three in phase one.

### Where it's worth spending

- **Drainage.** A patio that puddles is a patio nobody sits on, and fixing it later means tearing out the surface. Get this right the first time.
- **Lighting.** The difference between a patio that dies at sunset and one that fills up at 8pm. String lights are cheap and do most of the work — but have an electrician put them on a proper exterior circuit.
- **Shade orientation.** Georgia in July at 3pm is brutal. If the only available side faces west, the patio needs real afternoon shade or the happy-hour plan doesn't work. Walk the space at 4pm in summer before you decide where it goes.

## The four things that can stop a patio

Find out about all four in your first week of phone calls, before spending anything.

**1. Parking count.** Adding 20 seats can increase your required parking under Kennesaw's zoning. If you don't have the spaces, you need a variance, a shared-parking agreement, or a smaller patio. **This kills more restaurant patios than any other issue.** Ask Zoning first — it's question #3 in Call 2.

**2. Setbacks.** The patio has to sit a required distance from property lines. If the only space you have is right at the line, the footprint shrinks.

**3. ADA access.** An accessible route to the patio, accessible table heights, no step barriers. Federal, non-negotiable, and cheap if designed in — expensive if retrofitted.

**4. The enclosure spec for alcohol service.** Height, material, whether gates must self-close, whether it must be continuous. Get the exact requirement in writing **before** buying fence.

## Layout that actually works for service

```
            ── street side: visible, this is your advertising ──
     ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
     │  ▢ 4-top        ▢ 4-top        ▢ 4-top        │
     │                                                │  railing /
     │         ▢ 2-top          ▢ 2-top               │  fence line
     │  ▢ 4-top                       ▢ 4-top        │  = licensed
     │                                                │  premises edge
     └──────────────┬──────────┬──────────────────────┘
                    │  gate /  │  36"+ accessible route
                    │  service │
     ═══════════════╧══════════╧══════════════════════
          BUILDING — door near the service station
```

**Principles:**

- **One service door, short run.** Every extra step between the drink station and the farthest table is labor cost on every single round, forever. Put the door where the server walks least.
- **Tables visible from the street.** The patio's second job is advertising. Don't hide it behind a solid fence — use railing or slats people can see through. A visible full patio pulls cars in.
- **Keep the accessible route clear** and don't let servers treat it as a staging area.
- **Umbrella bases out of walkways.** Cheap problem to design around, annoying problem to live with.
- **Plan one spot for a speaker.** Low-volume music changes how long people stay, and how long they stay is the whole business model of a patio.

## Build sequence

```
Nov 2026   Walk the space at 4pm in summer heat. Decide the side.
           Three contractor quotes. Survey.
Dec 2026   Site plan drawn WITH the patio on it. Confirm parking math.
Jan 2027   Submit to Building Services. Submit alcohol application
           (Georgia Tax Center) with the patio on the premises plan.
Feb 2027   Permit issued → grade, drainage, surface.
Mar 2027   Railing, electrical, lighting, shade.
Apr 2027   Furniture. Final inspections. Updated Certificate of Occupancy.
May 2027   Soft open the patio on beer and micheladas while the liquor
           license finishes. Train service flow with real customers.
Jun 2027   Full open: patio + margaritas, into summer.
```

**The soft open in May is the most underrated step here.** Open the patio before the liquor license lands. You'll find out where the service bottleneck is, whether the umbrella placement works, whether tables wobble, and how long a round takes — using beer and micheladas, with no liquor liability, and no pressure. Then when margaritas arrive in June, your staff already knows how to run the patio.

## Phase two, for later

Only after phase one is paying for itself:

- Outdoor heaters or a fire feature → extends the season into December and February
- Retractable awning or pergola → rain insurance, which is real revenue on Georgia afternoons
- A second 10–15 seat section, if you're consistently turning people away
- A small outdoor service bar → cuts the walk to zero on busy nights
- A TV for Liga MX and World Cup matches → the single best dead-hour filler a taqueria can install

That last one deserves a note: **soccer schedules are dead-hour gold.** Liga MX, Champions League and World Cup qualifiers are often on weekday afternoons — exactly the hours you're trying to fill. A patio, a TV, micheladas, and a posted match schedule is a reliable way to turn 3pm Wednesday into a full house. It belongs in phase two only because of cost; strategically it's one of your strongest plays.
