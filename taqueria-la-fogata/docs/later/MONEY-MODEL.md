# The Money Model

Every number in this plan, where it came from, and how to replace it with yours.

> **Read this first.** The cost numbers below (what a bottle costs, what a lime costs) are solid industry figures and you can trust them within a few cents. The **volume** numbers — how many drinks you'll sell, how full the patio gets — are *my assumptions, not your data.* I've marked them **[ASSUMPTION]**. Replace every one with your real numbers as you learn them. A model built on your actual Tuesday is worth ten built on my guesses.

## Part 2: Drinks vs. food — why this matters so much

| | 3-taco plate | House margarita |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $12.00 | $10.00 |
| Cost | ~$3.60 | $1.68 |
| Gross profit | $8.40 | $8.32 |
| Gross margin | 70% | 83% |
| Kitchen labor | ~8 min cook + griddle | **none** |
| Who makes it | your cook | whoever is at the counter |
| Constrained at peak? | **yes** | no |

A margarita earns essentially the same gross profit as a taco plate while **using none of your scarcest resource.** During peak, your kitchen is the ceiling on your revenue. Drinks go around that ceiling entirely.

### The "same guest, one drink" lever

The cheapest revenue in any restaurant is getting the person already sitting there to add one thing.

**[ASSUMPTION]** Say you serve 60 guests on an average day and 1 in 4 adds a $10 margarita:

- 15 margaritas × $8.32 profit = **$125/day**
- × 6 days = **$749/week**
- = **~$3,245/month** = **~$39,000/year**

Nothing about that requires a single new customer. It requires a drink menu and a server who asks.

## Part 3: What the patio is worth

20 seats. Georgia patio season runs roughly March–November: call it **nine usable months.**

### Scenario A — weekday happy hour (the dead-hour fix)

Mon–Thu, 3:00–6:00pm. This window is currently near-empty, so almost all of this is new money.

**[ASSUMPTION]** 20 seats × 40% average occupancy × 1.5 turns over 3 hours = **12 covers/day**

Per cover: 1.5 margaritas at a $8 happy-hour price, plus $6 of food (chips and queso, a couple of tacos).

| | Per cover |
|---|---|
| Drinks: 1.5 × $6.32 profit | $9.48 |
| Food: $6.00 at 70% margin | $4.20 |
| **Gross profit per cover** | **$13.68** |

- 12 covers × 4 days = 48 covers/week → **$657/week**
- ~**$2,845/month** during season
- × 9 months = **~$25,600/year**

### Scenario B — weekend evenings on the patio

Fri–Sun, 5:00–9:00pm, full price.

**[ASSUMPTION]** 20 seats × 65% occupancy × 2 turns = **26 covers/day**

Per cover: 2 margaritas at $10, plus $16 of food.

| | Per cover |
|---|---|
| Drinks: 2 × $8.32 | $16.64 |
| Food: $16.00 at 70% | $11.20 |
| **Gross profit per cover** | **$27.84** |

- 26 covers × 3 days = 78 covers/week → **$2,172/week**
- ~**$9,400/month** during season
- × 9 months = **~$84,700/year**

### Combined, with a reality discount

Scenario A + B comes to roughly **$110,000/year in gross profit**. I don't want you planning on that, because first-year patios underperform while word spreads, weather kills days, and staffing a new service is bumpy.

**Apply a 50% haircut for year one: plan on ~$45,000–$55,000 of added gross profit.** If you beat it, excellent. If you plan on $110K and get $55K, you have a cash-flow problem instead of a success.

## Part 4: What it costs to get there

### Licensing and compliance (one-time + annual)

| Item | Estimate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kennesaw local alcohol license | **~$3,000/yr** | ⚠️ From a third-party aggregator, **not** confirmed with the city. Verify — this is the single number most worth a phone call. |
| Georgia state retail license | a few hundred | Via Georgia Tax Center, issued after the local license |
| Application / investigation / background fees | $200–600 | Kennesaw PD runs GCIC background checks |
| CPA food-sales affidavit (notarized) | $300–800 | Required by Kennesaw, annually |
| TIPS or approved alcohol workshop | ~$40/person | Required; on-premise option |
| Employee pouring permits | $50–75 each | Through Kennesaw PD |
| **Dram shop / liquor liability insurance** | **+$1,500–4,000/yr** | Get this quote **early**; it can surprise people |

The low end of this group assumes the ~$3,000 local license figure is right and you permit two servers. The high end assumes the local license comes in above $3,000, you permit five or six staff, and insurance lands at the top of its range. Until you've called the city, treat the whole group as $5,400–12,500 and don't plan tight against it.

## Part 5: Does it pay back?

Using the **discounted** year-one figure of $50,000 added gross profit against a $40,000 build:

| | |
|---|---|
| Added gross profit, year 1 (discounted) | $50,000 |
| Less annual license + insurance | –$6,000 |
| Less added labor (1 bartender-server, patio hours) | –$18,000 |
| **Net added profit, year 1** | **~$26,000** |
| Build cost | $40,000 |
| **Payback** | **~18 months** |
| Year 2 onward (no build cost, better volume) | **$35,000–60,000/yr** |

An 18-month payback on a restaurant capital project is **good**. Restaurant equipment is typically judged against a 2–3 year payback. And the patio is a permanent asset — it raises what the business is worth if you ever sell it.

### The stress test: what if it goes badly?

Half the volume I assumed, and the high end of the build cost:

| | |
|---|---|
| Added gross profit | $25,000 |
| Less license, insurance, labor | –$24,000 |
| **Net** | **~$1,000** |
| Build cost | $73,000 |

That is the bad case: you'd be roughly breaking even on operations and sitting on a $73,000 asset you can't pay back quickly. **This is why the build budget matters more than the revenue forecast.** You cannot control how many people sit on the patio in August 2027. You can completely control whether you spend $26,000 or $73,000 building it.

**The single most important financial decision in this project is keeping the build cheap.** Umbrellas instead of a pergola. Pavers instead of poured concrete if the grade allows. A service station instead of a full bar. Used furniture. Every dollar you don't spend on the build is a dollar you don't have to earn back.

## Part 6: The six numbers that decide this

Track these starting now. Details in [06-TRACKING.md](../06-TRACKING.md).

1. **Food % of total sales** — must stay above 50%. This is a license requirement, not a preference.
2. **Covers in the dead window (2–5pm)** — your baseline. You cannot prove the patio worked without it.
3. **Drinks per cover** — today it's beer-only. Watch it move as you add micheladas.
4. **Average ticket** — the number the whole plan is trying to raise.
5. **Michelada attach rate** — micheladas as a share of beers. You already sell these, so this is live evidence of whether your customers buy drinks, available now and for free.
6. **Where customers say they found you** — tells you whether Google or TikTok is doing the work.

## Replace my assumptions with yours

Everything marked **[ASSUMPTION]** is a guess I made so the model would run. The three that matter most, in order:

1. **Dead-window covers.** Count them for two weeks. If it's 4/day, not 12, the patio case is weaker and you should build cheaper. If it's 20, build bigger.
2. **Michelada attach rate.** You already sell micheladas, so this evidence already exists — go count it. If a solid share of your beer drinkers pay $8 for a michelada, the margarita case is strong. If almost nobody does, they may not pay $10 for a margarita either, and you want to know that *before* spending $40,000.
3. **Your real beer and lime invoice prices.** Pull last month's invoices and correct my per-unit costs. Takes 20 minutes and makes every table above yours instead of mine.
