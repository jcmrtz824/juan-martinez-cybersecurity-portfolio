# The Strategy: You Don't Have a Customer Problem, You Have a Clock Problem

**Taqueria La Fogata — Kennesaw, GA**
Target: liquor service + a 15–20 seat patio by summer 2027.

---

## Start here: what you actually told me

You said you are **busy at peak, dead the rest of the day.**

That one sentence changes the whole plan. It means:

- People already know you. They already like the food. You are not failing at attracting customers.
- Your rent, your insurance, your utilities, and at least some of your labor are **already paid for** during the dead hours. You are paying for 2pm–5pm whether anybody sits in it or not.
- So every dollar you earn in the dead hours is worth far more than a dollar earned at peak. At peak, a new dollar needs new capacity — a new table, a faster kitchen, another pair of hands. In the dead hours, a new dollar costs you almost nothing extra.

A marketing campaign that brings more people at noon on Saturday mostly creates a longer line. A campaign that brings people at 4pm on a Tuesday is nearly pure profit.

**So the goal is not "more customers." The goal is to turn your empty hours into a second business.**

---

## Why margaritas + a patio is exactly the right instinct

You picked the two upgrades that attack dead hours better than anything else on the menu. Here's why, in business terms.

### A patio creates a reason to stay, not just a reason to eat

A taqueria at 1pm is a transaction: get food, eat, leave. 20 minutes.

A patio at 5pm is an occasion: sit outside, have a drink, order another round of chips, stay an hour. In Georgia, you can run a patio comfortably from roughly March through November — that is about **nine months of usable season**, which is far better than the patio math works out to in most of the country.

A patio also sells itself from the street. A full patio is the best advertising a restaurant has ever had, and it is free. People driving down the road see occupied tables and read it as "that place is good." An empty dining room behind tinted glass tells them nothing.

### Drinks are the highest-margin thing you will ever sell, and they don't touch your kitchen

Two numbers from the full model in [04-MONEY-MODEL.md](./04-MONEY-MODEL.md):

| | Cost to make | Sells for | Gross profit | Kitchen time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3-taco plate | ~$3.60 | $12.00 | $8.40 | ~8 min of griddle + cook |
| House margarita | ~$1.68 | $10.00 | $8.32 | **0 min. Doesn't enter the kitchen.** |

A margarita makes nearly the same gross profit as a taco plate, in 90 seconds, with no cook, no griddle space, and no ticket time. During dead hours, when your kitchen is idle anyway, that's good. During peak, when your kitchen is the bottleneck, it is enormous — because drinks let you grow revenue **without** growing the thing that is already maxed out.

That is the real reason drinks matter to you specifically: **a drink menu raises your ceiling at peak and fills your floor at the dead hours, at the same time.**

> Build a patio and a short drink menu so that **3pm–6pm on a weekday becomes a business you didn't have before**, and so that the hours you're already full make more money per seat.

Everything in this folder serves that sentence.

## The one thing to do before anything else

You do not have to wait until 2027 to start making money on drinks.

**A michelada is beer-based.** You already sell beer. In most Georgia jurisdictions, mixing beer you are licensed to sell with non-alcoholic mixers — Clamato, lime, hot sauce, Worcestershire, a Tajín rim — is service of the beer you already have a license for, not service of a new category of alcohol.

Here's what that's worth:

- A domestic beer costs you about **$0.92** and sells for **$5.00**. Gross profit: **$4.08**.
- That same beer, built into a 16 oz michelada, costs you about **$1.60** and sells for **$8.00**. Gross profit: **$6.40**.

**You add $2.32 of profit to a beer you were already selling, for about 68 cents of mixers and a rim of chile salt.** No license. No permit. No construction. No new equipment beyond a shaker of Tajín and a bottle of Clamato.

If you sell 25 of them a week — a very low bar for a taqueria — that is **$58/week**, about **$3,000/year**, from a decision you can make this month. And every michelada you sell between now and 2027 is also market research: it tells you whether your customers want drinks, which is the single biggest unknown in the whole liquor plan.

**Confirm it with the City of Kennesaw Business License Office first.** One phone call, and there's a script for it in [02-GEORGIA-LIQUOR-LICENSE.md](./02-GEORGIA-LIQUOR-LICENSE.md). Do not take my word for a licensing question — take the city's.

## What could go wrong, honestly

Four real risks. None of them are reasons not to do this; all of them are reasons to do it in the right order.

**1. The 50% food rule.** Georgia defines an "eating establishment" as one that gets at least 50% of its food-and-beverage sales from food, and Kennesaw requires a **CPA-notarized affidavit** proving it. Kennesaw's earlier Sunday sales (11am instead of 12:30pm) are also gated on that same 50% threshold. A taqueria is naturally food-heavy, so you will almost certainly clear this — but it means you need to be **tracking the ratio monthly**, not discovering it once a year when the CPA asks. See [08-TRACKING.md](./08-TRACKING.md).

**2. Building the patio before the license knows about it.** Your liquor license covers a defined, approved *licensed premises*. If you get licensed for the indoor room and then add a patio, you have to go back and amend the premises — more time, more money, more review. The patio and the license need to be planned **together, from the first conversation with the city.** This is the most expensive mistake available to you in this project and it is completely avoidable.

**3. Liquor liability (dram shop) insurance.** The moment you pour spirits, your insurance changes and your legal exposure changes. Budget for it, get the quote early, and over-train your staff. Georgia holds you responsible for serving a visibly intoxicated person.

**4. A menu that's too big.** The temptation with a new bar is 14 cocktails. Don't. Launch with **four drinks**: house margarita, spicy/mango margarita, michelada, and a paloma. Four drinks means your staff is fast and consistent on day one, your inventory is three bottles, and nothing dies in the well. You add a fifth only when a customer asks twice.

## A note on where the money comes from

Nothing in this plan depends on you finding new customers in a new town. It depends on three things you already control:

1. **Selling more to the people already walking in** (micheladas now, margaritas later).
2. **Being open for business during hours you're already paying rent on** (the patio, happy hour).
3. **Being findable** (Google Business Profile, a website, TikTok).

That is why this is a good plan and not a gamble. You are not betting on demand you don't have. You are harvesting demand you are currently throwing away at 3pm on a Wednesday.
